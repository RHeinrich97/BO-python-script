// Chapter 01

The core intuition

Before writing a line of code or looking at a formula, let us ask the real question: why do we need this?

Imagine an acylation in a ball mill. You play with three parameters: temperature, reaction time, equivalents of acyl chloride. If you test 10 values per parameter, that is 103 = 1,000 reactions. Bayesian Optimization (BO) often reaches very good conditions in 15-30 experiments.

🏔
// The mountain analogy

Looking for the best yield is like finding the summit of a mountain in the fog. With each step you update a mental map of the terrain. BO does this rigorously: it estimates the whole topography from your few measurements, and suggests the most promising place to explore next.

The five-step cycle

1
Initial experiments

You run a few experiments covering the space (OFAT or random). Rule of thumb: at least 2 × (number of parameters). For 3 parameters, 6 experiments minimum.

2
Train the surrogate model (GP)

A Gaussian Process (GP) learns the relationship conditions -> results. It predicts not only a value, but also its uncertainty: where it has data versus where it does not know.

3
Compute the acquisition function

A score is computed for every untested point, combining prediction (exploitation) and uncertainty (exploration). The script maximises this score to find the best suggestion.

4
Go to the lab

You test the suggested conditions, measure the result, and enter it in the CSV (or in the app).

5
Update and repeat

The new point is added to the dataset, the GP is retrained, the next suggestion is better. Convergence is detected automatically.

💡
// Why it beats OFAT

OFAT varies one parameter at a time and assumes they are independent. BO explores the interactions: "does the effect of temperature depend on the solvent?" These interactions are often crucial in chemistry.

The r.r. ratio is not linear. The difference between 2:1 and 10:1 is far more significant than between 90:1 and 100:1. If you want to optimise regioselectivity, use ln(ratio) as the value in the CSV rather than the raw ratio. The GP will learn much better.

// Chapter 03

The surrogate model: the Gaussian Process

The heart of BO is the Gaussian Process (GP). Its particularity: it predicts not a fixed value but a distribution: a mean μ(x) and a variance σ2(x).

🎲
// Intuition

An honest colleague does not say "exactly 73.4 %". They say "between 65 and 80 %, I think around 72". The GP is that honest colleague. And its error bar is small where it has data, large where it explores.

Interactive demo - add points, watch the GP

The Matérn 5/2 kernel and ARD lengthscales

The script uses a Matérn 5/2 kernel with ARD (Automatic Relevance Determination): one lengthscale ℓi per dimension.

k(x, x') = σ2(1 + √5×d + 5d2/3) × exp(−√5×d)
where d = √Σi (xi − x'i)2 / ℓi2

Key takeaway: a small lengthscale ℓi -> the yield changes fast with this parameter -> highly influential parameter. This is the information the script uses to choose the axes of the GP landscape plots (the 2 parameters with the shortest ℓ).
🔬
// What it tells you about your chemistry

If the ligand type comes out with a small lengthscale, that is mechanistic information: the ligand is the main lever. If temperature has a large lengthscale, the reaction is not very sensitive to temperature in your range: you may be able to fix it.

// Chapter 04

The acquisition function

The acquisition function α(x) answers: "where am I most likely to beat my current best result?" It balances exploitation (promising regions) and exploration (unknown regions).

EI(x) = (μ(x) − f*) × Φ(z) + σ(x) × φ(z)
where z = (μ(x) − f*) / σ(x), f* is the best observed result,
Φ = normal CDF, φ = normal PDF.

First term: exploitation (regions where μ is high). Second term: exploration (regions where σ is high). EI = 0 at already observed points.
EI
Expected Improvement
Recommended by default. Natural exploration/exploitation balance. Parameter xi: 0.01 (exploitation) to 0.1 (exploration).
UCB
Upper Confidence Bound
Score = μ + κ×σ. Direct control via ucb_kappa: κ=1 exploits, κ=3 explores. Useful early in the campaign.
PI
Probability of Improvement
Probability of beating f*. More conservative than EI. Small safe gains rather than large risky jumps.
Thompson
Thompson Sampling
Draws a random scenario from the GP posterior. Best choice for batches (n_suggestions ≥ 2). Natural diversity.
🧭
// Recommended strategy

Start of campaign (iterations 1-5): UCB with κ=2.5 or EI with ξ=0.05, explores more.
Middle (iterations 6-15): EI by default (ξ=0.01), balanced.
End (refinement): EI or PI with ξ=0.001, exploits.
Batch ≥ 2: Thompson, automatic diversity.

If the gold circles are above the filled dots: the GP was too optimistic (it overestimated). If the circles are close to the filled dots: the GP is well calibrated, its predictions are reliable. A good sign of model maturity.

Style of reference Figure 2c. Two side-by-side panels:

PanelWhat it showsHow to read it

The script automatically picks the 2 continuous parameters whose ARD lengthscales are the shortest: those for which the yield changes fastest. If your ligand does not appear on the map, the GP gives it little influence in the tested range. That is useful chemical information in itself.

Final plots - *_FINAL_*.png

Generated automatically at convergence (or after convergence_patience rounds without improvement). Same as the per-round plots, at high resolution (dpi=200). Directly usable in a report or a publication.

Multi-objective: yield, ee, selectivity

In asymmetric chemistry you rarely optimise a single number. Several strategies are available via multi_objective_mode.

The Pareto front - the key idea

// Pareto front - Yield vs %ee

Mode 1 - weighted_sum

Score = w1×output1 + w2×output2. Simple, intuitive. Weights are normalised automatically.

YAMLmulti_objective_mode: "weighted_sum"
outputs:
  - name: "Yield (%)"
    direction: maximize
    weight: 0.6    # 60% of the importance
  - name: "ee (%)"
    direction: maximize
    weight: 0.4    # 40% of the importance
⚠️
// Limitation

Cannot explore the concave regions of the Pareto front. If yield and ee are strongly anticorrelated, some optimal solutions stay invisible.

Mode 2 - pareto (ParEGO)

Draws a random weight vector at each round (Dirichlet distribution). Progressively explores the whole Pareto front with no prior.

YAMLmulti_objective_mode: "pareto"
# Weights declared in outputs are ignored in pareto mode
# Uses an augmented Chebyshev scalarisation (ParEGO)
🗺️
// When to use it

When you want to map the front before choosing. After 15-20 rounds you will clearly see the available trade-offs, then you can switch to weighted_sum or threshold.

Mode 3 - threshold

Some objectives must meet a threshold before the rest is optimised.

YAMLmulti_objective_mode: "threshold"
outputs:
  - name: "Yield (%)"
    direction: maximize
    weight: 1.0          # main objective: maximise
  - name: "ee (%)"
    direction: maximize
    weight: 0.0
    threshold: 90         # constraint: ee must first be ≥ 90 %

Mode 4 - ehvi New in v2.3

MC Expected Hypervolume Improvement. Directly maximises the dominated volume of the Pareto front: theoretically superior to ParEGO, especially with 3+ objectives. Implemented in pure numpy (no extra dependency). This is the mode used by the desktop app as soon as a campaign has two or more objectives.

YAMLmulti_objective_mode: "ehvi"
bo_settings:
  ehvi_n_mc: 64           # MC samples (32=fast, 128=thorough)
  n_suggestions: 1        # recommended: 1 to limit computation time
  # ref_point:             # auto-computed if absent
  #   - 0.0                #   one value per objective
Criterionweighted_sumpareto (ParEGO)thresholdehvi
Config complexitySimpleSimpleMediumSimple
Explores concave frontNoYesNoYes
Theoretical qualityBasicGoodGoodBest
Time/suggestion~5s~5s~5s5-90s

1. 10 well-spread initial experiments.
2. Mode pareto or ehvi for 10-15 rounds -> map the front.
3. Analyse the trade-offs. Choose your priority.
4. Switch to weighted_sum or threshold for the last refinement rounds.

Automatic convergence & benchmark

Automatic convergence detection

After each round, the script checks whether the best score improved by more than 0.1 % over the last convergence_patience BO rounds.

converged if: (best_now − best_N_rounds_ago) / range < 0.001
N = convergence_patience (default: 5)
range = max(scores) − min(scores) observed

Value of 5: recommended by Shields et al. (JACS 2021) and Griffiths & Hernández-Lobato (2020) for small-budget chemistry campaigns (<50 experiments). Increase to 8-10 for longer campaigns.

At convergence, the terminal shows:

Terminal  [CONVERGENCE] Aucune amélioration depuis 5 rounds BO.
  [GRAPHIQUE] FINAL convergence: campagne_test_FINAL_convergence.png
  [GRAPHIQUE] FINAL paysage: campagne_test_FINAL_landscape.png

  -> La campagne semble avoir convergé.
     Si vous souhaitez continuer, augmentez 'convergence_patience'
🔁
// Continuing despite convergence

In the YAML, increase convergence_patience: 8 or change the acquisition function (UCB with kappa=3) to force exploration. Convergence is a signal, not a lock: you decide when to stop.

Benchmark of acquisition functions

Compares EI, UCB, PI, Thompson and Random on your existing data, without going back to the lab.

bashpython bo_chemistry.py --benchmark
# or explicitly:
python bo_chemistry.py config_test.yaml --benchmark
⚙️
// How the benchmark works

1. An oracle GP is trained on all your available data: it acts as a lab simulator.
2. For each acquisition function and each repetition: start again from the initial data and simulate N BO iterations by querying the oracle.
3. Result: curves best mean score ± standard deviation per function, like Figure 3 of the reference publications.

YAML configuration:

YAMLbo_settings:
  benchmark_n_iter: 10   # simulated iterations (more = more informative, slower)
  benchmark_n_rep: 5    # repetitions for the mean curve

  • My CSV has at least 2 × (number of parameters) rows with statut = fait

  • Categorical variables have all their categories declared in the YAML
  • Min/max bounds are chemically realistic (T < solvent bp, etc.)
  • I chose the right multi-objective mode for my priorities (see table Ch. 08)

// Chapter 11

Automatic detection
of stagnation - v2.5

New in v2.5: the script automatically detects when a continuous variable stays stuck on the same value for several rounds, and switches to Thompson (n=3) to escape the plateau, with no manual intervention.

🔍
// The concrete problem

Imagine the only good point of your campaign is at t=80 min. All following suggestions land around t≈79-82 min. The GP is convinced this is the optimum, but it has never seen a good result at t=95 min with the right other conditions. It cannot invent this information. Stagnation is detected, Thompson takes over.

// Why Thompson + 3 suggestions

Thompson draws independent scenarios from the GP posterior. With 3 suggestions, the probability that at least one escapes the plateau is statistically much higher than with a single one. The script automatically returns to your original settings as soon as stagnation disappears. You change nothing in the YAML.

⚠️
// Stagnation ≠ convergence

Stagnation of a variable does not mean the campaign has converged. It means the GP no longer explores that dimension. Score convergence is handled separately by convergence_patience. The two mechanisms are independent and complementary.

Key takeaway on initial points

Stagnation almost always happens because the initial points do not cover each dimension uniformly. If all your good initial points are at t=80 min, the GP will anchor its suggestions at t≈80 min regardless of the quality of the acquisition. The best prevention: use Latin Hypercube Sampling for the initial points, or make sure each variable covers at least 3 distinct levels spread over its range.

📐
// Rule of thumb

For 3 variables, each with a range: put at least 1 point in each third of each variable, independently. 3 thirds × 3 variables = a well-spread plan of 9 initial points. This matters more than any acquisition setting.

Directional drift
& LHS - v2.6

The problem stagnation does not solve

Stagnation (v2.5) detects when a variable stays still. But there is another equally common trap: the variable drifts in one direction while the score does not improve: the GP overshoots the optimum and keeps going the same way, for lack of data on the other side.

📈
// Real observed example

The GP saw t=124 min → 61%. It suggests t=128 then t=134 , always longer, without ever coming back to t=110 min (the true optimum). The score stalls around 60-61%. Drift is detected and the script switches to UCB kappa=0.2 to bring the suggestions back towards the maximum of μ(x), not towards uncertain regions.

MechanismDetects whenAction
Stagnation v2.5Variable still: low stdThompson + n=3
Drift v2.6Variable drifts + score stallsUCB kappa=0.2, n=1
🔒
// Safety condition

Drift is triggered only if the score is not improving (improvement < 0.5%). If the score keeps rising despite the drift, the script lets the GP continue: the optimum may really lie in that direction. This is what distinguishes real drift from normal progress.

Configuration YAML

YAMLbo_settings:

  drift_patience: 3          # rounds before detection
  drift_slope_threshold: 0.03  # 3% of the range per round

Most plateaus and drifts come from a poor initial design. The command --init-doe automatically generates a Latin Hypercube design guaranteeing that no dimension is under-represented. Since v3.9 it is a maximin LHS (30 candidate designs, the one with the largest minimum distance between points is kept), and points violating hard constraints are replaced by feasible ones.

How xi and kappa influence the suggestions

The two key parameters control the same fundamental trade-off:

FormulasEI:   score(x) = E[max(f(x) - f_best, 0)]
               high xi  ->  lowered fictitious f_best  ->  wider search region

UCB:  score(x) = mu(x) + kappa * sigma(x)
               kappa = 0  ->  follows mu only (pure exploitation)
               kappa ∞    ->  follows sigma only (pure exploration)

💡
// Practical memo

EI xi: imagine that the "bar to beat" drops in proportion to xi. With a high xi, any slightly better point counts: the GP explores further.

UCB kappa: it is the risk premium you grant to uncertainty. kappa=0 = ultra-conservative investor (follows the prediction). kappa=3 = adventurer (seeks unknown regions).

LHS vs factorial designs - clarification

⚠️
// LHS ≠ full factorial design

A full 2^k factorial design tests each variable at 2 levels (high/low): for 3 variables -> 23 = 8 experiments at the 8 corners. It explores neither the centre nor the intermediate regions, and the corners are often the least interesting regions chemically.

The LHS with N points guarantees that no dimension is under-represented, by covering the whole range uniformly. For 3 variables and N=6: 6 experiments spread over the whole range, not only the corners.

How the acquisitions work
in detail

The inner mechanics of acquisition functions

All acquisition functions answer the same question: at which point x of the space should I run the next experiment? They combine two pieces of information the GP provides for any untested point:

SymbolNamePhysical meaning
μ(x)GP meanThe best estimate of the yield at x: what the model expects to find there
σ(x)GP standard deviationThe model's uncertainty at x: low near the data, high in unexplored regions
 
Full formulasEI (Expected Improvement) :
  Z   = (μ(x) - f_best - xi) / σ(x)
  EI  = σ(x) × [Z × Φ(Z) + φ(Z)]
  Φ = normal CDF  φ = normal PDF  xi = exploration parameter
  -> Computes the EXPECTED improvement over the best known point.
  -> xi=0: a minimal improvement is enough   xi=0.1: looks for a large improvement

UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) :
  UCB = μ(x) + kappa × σ(x)
  -> Premium granted to uncertainty. kappa=0 = follows μ only. kappa=3 = follows σ.

PI (Probability of Improvement) :
  PI  = Φ((μ(x) - f_best - xi) / σ(x))
  -> RAW probability of beating f_best. More conservative than EI (ignores the magnitude).

Thompson Sampling :
  f̃(x) ~ GP(μ, K)   then   x* = argmax f̃(x)
  -> Draws a random scenario from the GP posterior and optimises on it.
  -> Naturally diverse for batches: each draw gives a different x*.
🏔
// Mountain-in-the-fog analogy

EI says: "I go where I have the best chance of improving , either because it is high (high μ), or because it is mysterious (high σ), or both."
UCB says: "I go where the upper bound of my confidence interval is highest: optimistic by nature."
Thompson says: "I imagine one possible scenario of the terrain and go to its summit: probabilistic exploration."

Experimental noise in the GP

Every GP includes a noise parameter alpha (or nugget) that represents the variance of the experimental noise, assumed uniform over the whole space. This assumption is often wrong in chemistry.

⚗️
// Homoscedastic vs heteroscedastic noise

Homoscedastic (assumed by the standard GP): same noise everywhere: 0.1% error at 10% yield AND at 90%.

Heteroscedastic (chemical reality): low noise near the optimum (reproducible reaction), high noise in marginal regions (poorly controlled reaction).

Consequence: the GP over-smooths noisy regions and under-fits clean regions. Predictions are less reliable. Partial solution: the Box-Cox transformation.

Box-Cox transformation - stabilising the noise

The Box-Cox transformation is a family of monotonic transformations that makes the output distribution more Gaussian and the noise more uniform. 

Box-Cox formulay_bc = (y^λ - 1) / λ    if λ ≠ 0
y_bc = log(y)           if λ = 0

Common special cases:
  λ = 0     -> logarithmic transformation (for very skewed data)
  λ = 0.5   -> square root (for counts, Poisson-like)
  λ = 1     -> identity (no transformation, data already Gaussian)
  estimated -> maximises the likelihood, automatic in the script
📊
// What it changes in practice

Typical distribution of chemical yields: right-skewed (many values at 0-30%, few at 80-100%). Box-Cox with λ≈0.1-0.3 compresses large values and stretches small ones, making the distribution more symmetric.

The GP is trained on the transformed values, then predictions are converted back to the real scale. No parameter to tune: λ is estimated automatically. Enable with boxcox: true in the YAML.

YAMLbo_settings:
  boxcox: true     # enable the Box-Cox transformation
  # lambda estimated automatically by maximum likelihood
  # condition: all output values must be > 0
  # if zeros exist -> automatic offset applied

Multimodality - detecting several optima

A chemical space is multimodal when several regions give high yields. This is common: the same substrate can react through two different mechanisms depending on the conditions, giving two "peaks" in the landscape. Classic BO finds one and exploits it: it may miss the other.

🏔🏔
// Multimodal example

In our test: false local peak at T=72°C, t=55 min, eq=1.5 (57%) and global optimum at T=47°C, t=110 min, eq=2.8 (100%). The GP could have locked onto the false peak and never explored the region of the global optimum.

Multimodal detection lists the other promising regions in the terminal at each round, without suggesting them automatically, because only the chemist can decide whether an alternative optimum makes physicochemical sense.

How detection works in the script

At each round, optimisation of the acquisition function starts from N random starting points (multi-start). Normally only the best is kept. Multimodal detection also keeps the TOP-K best distinct maxima (normalised distance > 0.15 between them):

The script can detect that the acquisition space is multimodal, but it cannot know whether the two peaks correspond to two real mechanisms or whether one is an artefact of a GP lacking data.   2. Replace the value of Xi with the grid value

Physicochemical encoding
of categoricals - v3.0

The problem with one-hot encoding

With classic one-hot encoding, the GP sees DMF = [1,0,0], DMAc = [0,1,0], DMSO = [0,0,1]: three orthogonal vectors, all at equal distance from each other. The GP does not know that DMF and DMAc are very similar (two amides, ε≈37) and cannot infer "if DMF works, DMAc should work too".

With physicochemical encoding, it sees DMF = [36.7, 3.82, 13.7, 11.3] and DMAc = [37.8, 3.72, 11.5, 10.2]: two points very close in descriptor space. The GP learns a continuous function over these dimensions and can interpolate.

EI-TS: the best of both worlds for batches

When n_suggestions ≥ 3, the classic Kriging Believer refits the GP entirely for each extra suggestion: heavy and redundant. EI-TS (after EDBO, Shields & Doyle, Nature 2021) adopts a hybrid strategy:

SuggestionMethodLogic
1stExpected ImprovementBest known exploitation
2nd...nThompson SamplingPosterior draw: natural diversity

Result: no refit between suggestions, 2-3× faster for n=3+, and batch diversity is ensured naturally by TS with no artificial constraint. 

YAMLbo_settings:
  acquisition: "EI-TS"   # Recommended as soon as n_suggestions ≥ 3
  n_suggestions: 3

UCB auto-β: automatic calibration of exploration

Our classic UCB uses a fixed kappa: one more parameter to tune. UCB-AUTO computes β from the theoretical bound of Srinivas 2010: 

Formulaβ = √(2 × log(d × t² × π² / 6δ))

d = number of dimensions (parameters)
t = current iteration        -> β grows with time
δ = 0.5 (theoretical constant)
📈
// Adaptive behaviour

At the start (small t): low β -> exploitation favoured. Later (large t): β grows -> exploration increases automatically. No need to choose kappa any more.

YAMLacquisition: "UCB-AUTO"   # No ucb_kappa to tune

VARMAX: forced pure exploration

Selects the point of maximum variance: where the GP is most uncertain. Last-resort mode if stagnation is detected and Thompson was not enough. Also useful to map a region of the landscape deliberately. 

YAMLacquisition: "VARMAX"   # Pure exploration, use occasionally

GPyTorch architecture
100% - v3.3 (updated v3.9)

Why move from sklearn to GPyTorch?

The sklearn GP worked well, but had two structural limitations that matter for small chemical datasets (5-15 points):

sklearn (v≤3.2)GPyTorch (v3.3+)
Regularisation Fixed bounds (0.01, 5.0) on the lengthscales GammaPrior(3, 0.5): a Bayesian prior that pulls the hyperparameters towards reasonable values
Noise alpha=0.5 fixed GaussianLikelihood: noise learned from the data
Optimisation DE + L-BFGS-B (deterministic) ADAM (gradient descent, 100 iters × 3 to 8 restarts depending on data size)
Thompson Sampling 4000×4000 covariance matrix (128 MB) rsample() Lanczos, memory efficient

GammaPriors - the real impact

The fixed bounds (0.01, 5.0) prevented the hyperparameters from diverging, but bluntly: a lengthscale at the bound was "stuck" with no information on which direction to take. The prior GammaPrior(3, 0.5) acts differently: it gently pulls towards its modal value (≈4 in raw space) while letting the data speak.

🎯
// Analogy

The sklearn bounds are a cage. The GPyTorch prior is a spring: it pulls back towards the centre if you drift too far, but leaves the freedom to adapt to the data when they are informative.

Learned noise - why it matters

In organic synthesis, experimental noise depends on the context: a reaction in a reactive extruder is very reproducible (σ ≈ 2-5%), an amidation in a manual vial less so (σ ≈ 5-10%). With GaussianLikelihood, the GP estimates this noise from your own data and adjusts its confidence accordingly, with no alpha to calibrate. Since v3.9 the prior on that noise follows the measurement reliability you declare (see below).

📌
// v3.9 fix: noise priors on the right scale

The GP is trained on standardised outputs (mean 0, variance 1). Up to v3.8, the noise prior had been tuned for raw yields (0-100): the "low" setting GammaPrior(1.1, 0.05) expected a noise variance of about 22 on a signal of variance 1. The GP therefore overestimated the noise by a factor of ~2 and smoothed away real peaks: on a test campaign it predicted 73.5 at a point measured at 81 (residual +7.5). With priors recalibrated for standardised y, the estimated noise fell from ±5.2 to ±1.7 yield points, the residual on that point to 1.0, and the GP learned the high-temperature decline it had been flattening.

Practical consequence: declare how reliable your measurements are. In the app this is the Measurement reliability menu; in the YAML, the top-level key noise_prior: low | medium | high (low noise = high reliability, e.g. careful HPLC or quantitative NMR).

The desktop app

app_bo.py opens a native window (pywebview) on top of the same engine. It writes the YAML and CSV for you, and adds: a guided creation form with presets, a summary of the initial design size before creation, campaign duplication (useful for a series of substrates), the configuration panel of a running campaign, interactive convergence, acquisition and Pareto plots, and CSV export. See the Installation & launch chapter.

Autopilot

Instead of switching acquisition functions by hand (ch. 13), the autopilot picks one at each round from three signals: progress (best result / target), coverage (fraction of the space explored) and stagnation (rounds without improvement). Thresholds scale with the number of dimensions.

PhaseWhenTypical acquisition
EARLYLittle data, low coverageUCB, high κ (explore)
MIDModel learningEI; UCB if stalled; EI-TS if coverage is too low
LATEClose to target with good coverage, or long stagnationEI with small ξ (refine)
 
YAMLbo_settings:
  autopilot: true
  autopilot_target: 95      # target value of the objective

In multi-objective campaigns the autopilot is off: EHVI handles the trade-off itself.

Experimental constraints

Two families, following Hickman, Aldeghi, Häse and Aspuru-Guzik, Digital Discovery 2022, 1, 732:

TypeApplies toKnownTypical use
HardInput parametersBefore the experimentEquipment limits, total equivalents, minimum total concentration, forbidden combinations
SoftA measured outputAfter the experimentKeep a by-product below a threshold while optimising the yield

Hard constraints restrict the acquisition to feasible points; the initial design and every suggestion are checked again before being returned. Soft constraints get their own GP, which estimates the probability of satisfying them and weights the acquisition.

YAMLconstraints:                       # Python expressions on parameter names
  - "eq_A + eq_B <= 4"
  - "eq_A + eq_B >= 2"
  - "not (Temperature > 90 and Solvent == 'THF')"

soft_constraints:
  - name: "by-product low"
    output: "By-product (%)"   # must be one of the outputs
    direction: minimize          # satisfied if output <= threshold
    threshold: 5

The app offers a guided builder for the common cases (bound, ratio between two parameters, sum of two parameters, limit on an output). Arbitrary expressions remain available through an imported YAML.

Logarithmic scale

For variables whose effect is log-linear (concentration, equivalents, catalyst loading, reagent ratio), the GP works better on log(x): 0.5 and 2 equivalents become symmetric around 1. You still enter and read normal values; only the internal encoding changes. Both bounds must be strictly positive.

YAML  - name: "Concentration (M)"
    type: continuous
    min: 0.05
    max: 1.0
    encoding: log_ratio

Initial design

The automatic number of initial points now counts categorical levels as well as continuous variables, and the LHS is maximin (best of 30 candidate designs). Example: 2 continuous variables and 5 solvents give 8 initial experiments instead of 5; 22 solvents would give 24, the cap. Ticking only the 4-6 solvents that make chemical sense keeps the design small.
